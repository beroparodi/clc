Light Up a Comet

Pick a comet, the ice that drives it, or two ices on separate patches, and how the Sun’s heat spreads over the nucleus. The standard model of a sublimating nucleus, as in How bright will a comet get? The physical magnitude law may tell, gives the share η of the absorbed sunlight that goes into sublimation; the physical law turns it into the magnitude in your aperture. Fit the physical and the empirical law to photometry, before and after perihelion if you like, compare the ices and test forecasts.

Each ice sublimates from its own patches with its own energy balance (Sec. VI D of the paper); k and 1 − k are the shares of the dust the two ices would supply if both were energy-limited, so Φ = kη1 + (1 − k)η2 + νr3/2 (Eq. 4). Fit finds k.

The empirical law m = m0 + 5 log Δ + 2.5 n log r (Eq. 2) is fitted (m0 and n) to the same points as the coma. “Before and after perihelion” fits both laws separately to the two arcs, as in Sec. IV of the paper. With a date, Fit and Compare use only earlier points; later ones (open circles) test both forecasts. In our test of 24 comets the physical law beats the empirical law with fitted n where its ice switches on within the fitted arc (7 of 9 comets, against 3 of 15 elsewhere), because the steep rise saturates; elsewhere it does no better than n = 3.5.

Constants of the paper: S⊙ = 1361 W m⁻², Bond albedo AB = 0.01, emissivity ε = 0.9, vapor pressures PX = AX e−BX/T and latent heats LX = BXkB/mX (Table I); energy balance Eq. (11), sublimation flux Eq. (13). K is the Afρ the comet would have at 1 au if all absorbed power went into sublimation; μ follows from K, HN, m⊙ and the aperture, and gives the constants of Eq. (4), m1 = HN − 2.5 log μ and ν = 10−0.4βNα/μ. The dust speed is taken ∝ r−1/2. A dust mantle of conductance h = κ/d splits the energy balance into one at the surface, (1−AB)S⊙⟨cos ζ⟩/r² = εσTs⁴ + h(Ts − Ti), and one at the ice front, h(Ts − Ti) = ZX(Ti)LX. Orbits are two-body conics with a low-precision Earth, accurate to a few 0.01 au.
